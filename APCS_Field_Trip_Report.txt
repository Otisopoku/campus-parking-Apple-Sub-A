Field Trip Laboratory Report: Aytech Power Controls System (APCS), Dadeban – Accra
Date: 25 March 2026
Programme: Engineering Technology (Computer Engineering)
Location: APCS Automation, Dadeban – Accra

INTRODUCTION
On 25 March 2026, I took part in an industrial visit to Aytech Power Controls System (APCS) in Dadeban, Accra. The visit was meant to strengthen our understanding of how automation is implemented in real facilities—moving beyond classroom theory into practical engineering workflows such as control panel design, relay logic, PLC programming concepts, instrumentation, and troubleshooting.

The engineers emphasized a key idea: automation starts with understanding the process (what the plant must do and in what sequence), then translating that into a control strategy that is safe, reliable, and easy to maintain. This made the trip especially relevant to me as a computer engineering student because industrial automation relies heavily on digital logic, structured programming, and industrial communication between sensors, controllers, and actuators.

OBJECTIVES OF THE TRIP
- Connect classroom lessons on PLCs, motor control, and circuit diagrams to industrial practice.
- Understand how relay-based control relates to PLC systems.
- Observe training equipment used for PLC/HMI and panel wiring.
- Learn the purpose of common instrumentation devices and how they support control.
- Gain exposure to the engineering approach to fault diagnosis and problem-solving.

ACTIVITIES AND TRAINING SESSIONS
1) Logic, process understanding, and control panels
The session began by explaining that an “application” in industry is simply a process that must be understood before it can be automated. We were taught that industrial control is built on logic that produces predictable outcomes, often expressed as ON/OFF states or 1/0. Using practical examples, the facilitators linked this to how relay systems use relays, contacts, and timers to implement decisions.

They stressed that being able to read and interpret circuit/schematic diagrams is essential, because the same schematic guides the construction of a control panel and becomes the main reference for troubleshooting faults. This tied directly to our school lab experience with electrical diagrams, but here it was presented as a real workplace requirement.

2) PLC concepts and the scan cycle
After the relay foundation, we moved to PLCs and learned that a PLC (Programmable Logic Controller) is a controller designed to execute process logic through programming. Compared to heavily wired control, PLC-based systems are generally easier to modify and expand because changes can often be made in software.

We discussed the PLC scan cycle, where the controller repeatedly reads inputs from sensors and switches, executes programmed logic, and updates outputs to actuators such as lamps, contactors, valves, and drives. We also learned that common industrial PLC brands include Siemens, Allen‑Bradley, and Schneider, which are widely used due to reliability and strong support for different scales of application.

We observed training setups including PLC training panels, control boards, and HMI interfaces, which made the idea of input/output mapping and operator control much more concrete.

3) Motor control context (why regulation matters)
The engineers related motor control to typical processing tasks such as pumping/transfer of liquids, mixing/stirring, and controlled motion on conveyor lines. We discussed why motors must be operated within defined limits (including frequency constraints around 50 Hz in standard environments) and how poor control can shorten equipment life or destabilize a process. This reinforced that automation is not only “turn it on,” but ensuring the motor runs at the correct speed and sequence for the process.

4) Instrumentation: measuring and sending signals for control
A major part of the visit focused on instrumentation—devices that measure process variables and provide feedback for control. Using a process-flow example, we identified:
- Flow meter: measures flow rate of a fluid (gas, water, oil, etc.).
- Pressure gauge: local indication of pressure.
- Pressure transmitter: measures pressure and sends an electrical signal that a controller can interpret.
- Control valve (positional valve): regulates flow by opening to controlled positions (e.g., 25%, 50%, 75%, 100%).

A key takeaway was the difference between instruments that only display values and instruments that transmit signals for automation. We also discussed abnormal conditions such as high pressure and how control strategies use feedback and protective elements so the system can regulate or respond safely when operating conditions exceed limits.

5) Engineering mindset and career-relevant skills
The facilitators repeatedly emphasized that engineers are problem solvers. When a system fails, the practical approach is to return to the process logic and schematic, isolate the fault, and implement a solution that restores safe operation. They also noted that companies often value skills—PLC knowledge, instrumentation awareness, and industrial communication—more than a specific degree title.

For computer engineering in particular, the session highlighted how PLC programming and industrial communication benefit from strong foundations in logic design, programming thinking, and structured troubleshooting.

KEY TECHNICAL LESSONS LEARNED
- Automation begins with process understanding, then logical design, then implementation.
- Schematics are essential tools for building and troubleshooting control panels.
- PLCs operate through a repeating input → logic → output scan cycle.
- Instrumentation provides the measurements needed for reliable feedback control.
- Control valves enable proportional regulation rather than all-or-nothing control.
- Accuracy in wiring and measurement matters because small errors can create major faults.

CONCLUSION
The APCS visit was a practical extension of what we study in class. Seeing relay logic, PLC training systems, HMI panels, and instrumentation devices in one environment helped me understand how individual topics combine into a complete automation system.

Overall, the trip improved my confidence in reading schematics, thinking in control logic, and understanding how sensors and transmitters support PLC-based decision-making. It also reinforced that engineering success depends on hands-on competence, clear reasoning, and the ability to troubleshoot systematically.
